    Why it barely moves. Retailer Ordering is rated as high as or higher than Demand Sensing, Inventory Visibility and Rural Warehousing on every single criterion, so no weighting lets those three win. Route Optimization can only win if the time-to-value weight is bigger than the data weight plus twice the KPI-breadth and scale weights. With our weights that is 15% against 115%.

    Ratings we used

    The bigger reason is dependency. NesMitra produces the village-level signal that forecasting, replenishment and route planning all depend on. Try it on the Dashboard tab: turn on DECIDE without SEE, and accuracy stays near 58% while service stays near 40%.

    What we simulate

    One rural territory, simulated one day at a time for 26 weeks after a 28-week warm-up. A regional DC supplies a town node (the distributor), which serves 30 villages directly or through a Rural Relay. Each village sells five product groups from the case, and anything not on the shelf is a lost sale.

    Each scenario is run 8 times with different random villages and demand. The cards show the average and the lowest and highest of the 8 runs. The same settings always give the same numbers, so the demo can be repeated exactly.

    What each switch changes inside the model

    SwitchWhat it changesSlide
    TodayVillage orders come from averages by village type: half the annual plan, half recorded sales, which stock-outs keep low. Fixed days-of-stock norms everywhere. The distributor indents every two weeks. One van per village, and many planned visits don't happen. No rule on which batch goes out first.1
    NesMitraEach shop gets a suggested order: expected sales until the next delivery, plus safety stock, minus what is on the shelf. The app also records missed sales, so demand stops looking smaller than it is. Shops change suggestions by up to 25%. Shops not on the app get assisted orders, which carry a weaker signal. App, support and field-team costs are charged.9
    Plan by typePremium is stocked only where it has proven demand; elsewhere it goes against confirmed orders. Impulse comes in small lots, and only against orders in R3. Beverages follow the season. Each product group gets its own service factor.4
    ForecastA seasonal forecast built from village demand, with sparse Impulse histories pooled. The distributor indents weekly and, trusting the forecast, holds two weeks instead of nine.5, 6
    Safety stockBuffers are sized from demand and lead-time variability and held mainly at the town node, where combined variability is lower. Shelves hold what sells until the next delivery, plus a small buffer.7
    FEFOThe oldest batch that will still sell in time goes out first, to the fastest-selling shops. Stock about to expire at slow shops is picked up and sent where it will sell.7
    Rural RelayOrders close before the route day. Then one run serves the whole route: R1 direct twice a week, R2 by mini-truck from the Relay, R3 through a market-day partner. Failed runs go out again the next day with a backup partner. No stock is kept at the Relay. Extra handling cost and damage are charged.8

    Does "Today" match the case?

    Working it out...

    Service, accuracy, cover and expiry are matched to the case's network-wide numbers. Cost-to-serve is matched to each cluster's own index (B 1.3x, A 1.8x), since the model is a single territory.

    How each target is measured here

    ServiceUnits sold divided by units wanted at village shelves
    Forecast accuracy1 minus WAPE of the weekly forecast per route and product group, against true demand. In real life you can only compare with sales; the model can also see the sales that were missed.
    CoverStock at the distributor, on shelves and in transit, divided by average daily demand
    Expiry and damageExpired plus damaged units, divided by everything that left the network
    Cost-to-serveAll costs per unit sold, divided by the urban cost per unit. It includes operating costs outside the tested switches (held fixed) plus app and Relay running costs. The costs are made up, so read it as direction and trade-off, not a measured saving.

    What the model doesn't show

    • Forecast methods. The model forecasts with simple smoothing, a known seasonal pattern and pooling for Impulse. It does not test the ETS, ARIMA, SARIMAX or machine-learning candidates on Slide 5; 78% is the accuracy of this simple setup.
    • FEFO. We tested it with heavy old stock (35% of each delivery arriving with 62 to 82% of its shelf life gone). With CircuitX on, it made no measurable difference, because stock turns in about 22 days. In Today, it trimmed expiry and damage from 4.1% to 3.8%. Here, fast flow does the freshness work; FEFO stays as a control for slow and seasonal stock.
    • Planning by type. It isn't needed to meet the five targets on average, and it shifts them only slightly (switching it off moves service from 88.0% to 88.4%). Its value is product-level fit and risk, which averages hide.
    • The map. It shows one of the 8 territories as a schematic. Village positions and Relay points are illustrative, not geography or route optimisation.
    • Rural Relay. The result covers the whole delivery package: grouped orders, fixed days, fewer failed runs and a next-day backup. It doesn't isolate the value of the Relay point itself.
    • Adoption. Each shop joins the app with the chosen probability, so the actual share differs between territories. The 40% and 60% levels are model findings, not field thresholds.
    • Gold Cells. They differ from wider A in two ways at once: the corridors are chosen (denser, nearer, higher potential) and the rules are reset. The result can't be credited to recalibration alone.
    What this is and isn't. It is a teaching model with made-up villages and costs. It shows why the five numbers move together and which combinations work. It doesn't predict CircuitX's results; the Cluster B pilot will, with real data, before any expansion.
